About This Course

Hello, I'm Paula Scher.
I’m a graphic designer, teacher, and artist. I’ve
worked as a designer for over 50 years, and
for the past 32 years, I’ve been a partner at
Pentagram Design in New York City.

several of the many forms of graphic design,

be a good designer. These lessons will inspire
you, and give you a better understanding

in relation to all forms of graphic design.

history, you’ll improve your work. And if you’re
curious about design, these lessons will help
you to see, and recognise design.

album art for hours. It was a mess of lines and

03. Origins of Type

Graphic design is one of the oldest
professions in the world. People needed
to communicate ideas to one another, and
they used symbols and images to do it, and
eventually typography.
The term was coined in the 1920s by designer
W.A. Dwiggins. Even so, the term didn’t gain
popularity until around the ‘60s and ‘70s.
Before then, graphic designers were called
commercial artists. But the job to create
messages through design existed long before.
Pictographs and hieroglyphics, used to tell
stories and messages, evolved into alphabets.

pictographs to the modern English alphabet.

column, which was built in 118 AD and featured

04. Crests, Flags, Symbols and Logos

In the Middle Ages and beyond, populations
used crests, flags, and symbols to convey
wealth, power, and social position. Flags
were used in war, in monarchy, and other
social positions. Their form and colour were
sometimes intricate, and sometimes quite
simple. In some instances, they told stories
about who they were representing. For
example, the image of a lion was a commonly
used symbol representing royalty, strength
and resilience. Flags were meant to be
distinct and easily recognisable.

05. TYPE!



What you see in typography
is nuance and form and spirit:
words have meaning, type has
spirit. And when you put them
together, the combination is
spectacular.
Typography expresses emotions. Type can
look heavy or light, eccentric or neutral,
classical or modern, romantic or powerful.
There are millions of typefaces available that
designers can use to express any emotion
they choose. They can also experiment with
creating their own fonts or even program them.
Illustrate with type
During art school, I had difficulties mastering
press type, the technology we used to
demonstrate type design. I remember
being frustrated by the process. Something
clicked when my teacher, Stanislaw Zagorski,
instructed me to illustrate with type. I realised
that type can have a specific character. A
designer could use typography to create
sensibility and messaging, the same way they
do with illustration or graphics.

Colour
Using colour in design can add or subtract
weight and emphasis to a specific piece of
work. You create focal points or draw attention
to certain areas. You can enhance a design’s
readability with use of colour.
The understanding of use of colour can
happen by looking at the work of other people
and from trial and error – try experimenting
with colours and failing and making
discoveries.
My husband, Seymour Chwast, is a graphic
designer and illustrator. His advice for
understanding colour is to look out for
something with colours you like and borrow
those colours and use them together in a
different composition that you created. You
will understand that colour relationships
change based on the composition.

The Secession movement* started in the
late 1800s but continued into the 20th
century. It featured long, elongated shapes.
Connected to this movement was the William
Morris style which was elegant and long, and
featured in fabric and home decor, as well as
book covers and posters. Josef Hoffmann, of
the Wiener Werkstätte collective in Germany,
used pattern in a much simpler way than the
artists of the Secession or Morris periods.
Curved form evolves to more complicated
forms and patterns, but with straighter lines.

Word and image for use in advertising was big
in this time. Ads featured big, bold typography
and were simplistic. Many of the designs in
this period feel contemporary, like the work of
Lucian Bernhard or the work he inspired in Italy.
Design became more illustrative and flatter in
the US during this period. And the concept of
a series emerged – people expected multiple
designs. The Harper’s Bazaar posters in the
States are an example of this.

Mumbai Airport
This airport serves millions of passengers
every day. The client knew they wanted the
experience of those millions of passengers to
be a strong sense of arrival to India – that this
couldn’t be any other airport in any other part
of the world.
So Michael Gericke worked with consultants,
took trips to India, researched, and learned.
In his research, he studied the Jali pattern
which was traditionally used to filter light into
buildings. He also noticed the motif of a dome,
a graphical shape that’s very specific to India.
Michael and his team did a deep exploration of
these forms, using laser and water machines
to cut and create different patterns.
As with the Toronto Pearson airport or
any wayfinding system, they needed to
plan for clear communication. Through
experimentation with colour and many
different materials, as well as collaboration
with the architectural and interiors team,
they finalised on a design that clearly
communicated the expression of Mumbai.

20. Film Titles and Animations in Practice

Title sequences for tv shows serve a similar
function to movie title sequences – they set
the viewer up with the tone, and sometimes
story, before getting into the show. Emily
noticed that TV title sequences had got shorter
lately. The hope, by making them shorter, is
that viewers won’t want to skip the intro. This
gives the designer less of an opportunity to be
expressive and is an added challenge. We have
a shorter amount of time to do our job – clearly
communicate the show’s title, relay a tone
or emotion, and tell a story in a beautiful and
interesting way. It’s a tall order for just a few
seconds.
Motion designers have come to rely on witty
devices to help with this. The title sequence
for Girls was the word ‘Girls’ in large type, for a
very short amount of time. But with every new
episode, the colour of the text changed, and
this variety became a point of interest.
The Killing Eve titles also changed with every
new episode - a single drop of blood that
dripped down from a different letter, with
every new episode.

Emily’s favourite things about motion are
simplicity and cacophony*. This is a lot like
scale to me – big and simple, or detailed and
complicated – anything in between is boring.
Her identity work with the Russo Brothers on
their film company, AGBO and its sub-brand,
Gozie Agbo, are perfect examples of these
polar opposites, and they’re both incredibly
successful.
The identity work Emily did for Serif, a social
networking space for LGBTQIA+, was inspired
by the community it serves, which, like all of
humanity, is varied and diverse. They developed
the brand with this in mind, but also in the
motion graphics they made. The way fluid
shapes and dots come together to tell the
story of the Serif community is reminiscent of
The Dot and the Line. Simple and effective, the
shapes feel alive and tell an engaging story.
The recognisability of a heartbeat in the
identity work Emily did for Kevin Hart’s
entertainment company, HartBeat, helps tell
an engaging story. There’s a reveal and an ‘Aha!’
moment when the viewer recognises the B-like
heart shape. The mark is simple and strong, and
can be used in a wide variety of applications.
